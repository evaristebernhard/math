\begin{abstract}
How many isolated periodic responses can a scalar equation with only three affine regions and a single sinusoidal forcing possess? For the symmetric saturation family
$\dot x=ax+(b-a)\sat(x)+\mu\sin t$, $ab<0$, five periodic solutions were known to occur, and five had been proposed as the universal maximum. We establish a lower bound of seven, using the explicit rational parameters
$(a,b,\mu)=(-9,3/2,181363/100000)$.
Beyond the witness, we explain the mechanism: increasing the mean of a periodic response shortens its lower exterior visit but lengthens its upper exterior visit. Their competing contributions to the total time spent in the expanding middle region can create an extra pair of folds in the mean-response curve, especially near grazing. A residence-time identity makes this competition precise and guided the discovery of the parameters. To separate the mechanism from its verification, we give both a numerical portrait of the folds and a computer-assisted proof using six rational shooting endpoints, exact affine flight maps, outward-rounded interval signs, and half-period symmetry. The central orbit is separately proved hyperbolic. The conclusion is \emph{at least} seven isolated periodic orbits, not a sharp global bound.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} piecewise-linear dynamics; limit cycles; periodic forcing; grazing; response curve; interval arithmetic. \quad

\section{The problem: why a three-zone equation is not necessarily simple}
Consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
\dot x=ax+(b-a)\sat(x)+\mu\sin t,
\qquad \sat(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&x<-1,\\ x,&|x|\le1,\\1,&x>1,\end{cases}
\qquad ab<0,\ \mu>0.
\end{equation}
A $2\pi$-periodic solution is a closed trajectory on the phase cylinder $(t\bmod 2\pi,x)$; it is a limit cycle when isolated among periodic trajectories on that cylinder. Different initial states at the same phase $t=0$ represent different orbits, even if their waveforms are similar.

The family is deceptively elementary. Each flight inside a fixed zone is explicitly solvable, but its \emph{duration} is determined by the unknown times at which the solution reaches $x=\pm1$. The number and ordering of these events can change at a grazing orbit, where a turning point lies exactly on a switching level. It is this moving switching geometry, rather than complexity of individual flights, that makes global cycle counting difficult.

Bravo, Carmona, Fern\'andez and Ojeda \cite{Bravo2025} showed uniform finiteness, constructed five-solution regimes, and posed five as a conjectural sharp upper bound. A separate sparse toric approach \cite{HuYuBound} bounds the full family by $16437$, leaving the exact maximum open. Here we disprove the proposed value five by a single, fully specified, reproducible example.

\section{The response curve turns cycle counting into a problem about residence time}\label{sec:response}
Write $A=-a>0$ and $B=b>0$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g}
g(x)=Ax-(A+B)\sat(x).
\end{equation}
We introduce an auxiliary constant bias $\lambda$ and study
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bias}
\dot x+g(x)=\mu\sin t+\lambda,\qquad x(t+2\pi)=x(t).
\end{equation}
The periodic responses of \eqref{eq:bias} can be parametrized globally by their mean
$\xi=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}x(t)\,dt$; write $\lambda=\Lambda(\xi,\mu)$ and $x=x(t;\xi,\mu)$. Strict scalar order implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:z}
z(t):=\partial_\xi x(t;\xi,\mu)>0,
\end{equation}
as shown in \cite[Section~2]{HuYuBound}. The original, unbiased equation corresponds exactly to the zeros of $\Lambda(\cdot,\mu)$. Since $g$ is odd and $\sin(t+\pi)=-\sin t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:odd}
\Lambda(-\xi,\mu)=-\Lambda(\xi,\mu),\qquad \Lambda(0,\mu)=0.
\end{equation}
Thus the zero at the origin gives the central antisymmetric orbit, and every positive noncentral zero has a distinct negative partner.

\subsection{An exact derivative identity}
The decisive observable is the residence time
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
T(\xi,\mu)=\operatorname{meas}\{t\in[0,2\pi): |x(t;\xi,\mu)|<1\}.
\end{equation}
Away from the finitely many switching instants, differentiate \eqref{eq:bias} with respect to $\xi$. Since $z>0$,
\[
\dot z+g'(x)z=\Lambda_\xi,
\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\log z+g'(x)=\frac{\Lambda_\xi}{z}.
\]
Integration over one period cancels the logarithmic boundary term. Writing $J=\int_0^{2\pi}z^{-1}(t)\,dt>0$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:key}
\boxed{\ \Lambda_\xi(\xi,\mu)\,J(\xi,\mu)=(A+B)(\rho-T(\xi,\mu)),\qquad
\rho=\frac{2\pi A}{A+B}.\ }
\end{equation}
Indeed $g'=-B$ in the middle and $g'=A$ in the two exterior zones. The identity also extends to the grazing boundary by continuity as detailed in \cite{HuYuBound}.

Equation \eqref{eq:key} is the essential reduction: the response curve slopes down when $T>\rho$ and up when $T<\rho$. It explains why an upper bound of five looked plausible. If the negative-slope portion on $\xi>0$ had a sufficiently simple connected shape, $\Lambda$ could cross zero at most twice after the central crossing. The example below shows that this shape property fails.

The same residence time determines the Floquet multiplier of an \emph{unbiased} periodic solution. If $P(y)$ denotes the full-period return map at phase zero, its derivative at a periodic point is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplier}
P'(y)=\exp\left(-\int_0^{2\pi}g'(x(t))\,dt\right)
=\exp\bigl((A+B)(T-\rho)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus the threshold $T=\rho$ simultaneously marks a response fold and the change from $P'<1$ to $P'>1$. One need not solve a separate variational equation to determine a multiplier after the time spent in each zone is known.

\subsection{Why the two exterior zones compete}
On a four-crossing orbit, let $s$ be the time in the lower exterior zone $x<-1$, and let $v$ be the time in the upper exterior zone $x>1$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compete}
T=2\pi-s-v,\qquad T_\xi=-s_\xi-v_\xi.
\end{equation}
Increasing the mean raises the trajectory pointwise. Consequently the lower visit shrinks ($s$ decreases) and the upper visit expands ($v$ increases). The two terms in \eqref{eq:compete} have \emph{opposite effects} on $T$. There is no reason for their balance to have a universal sign.

Near a nondegenerate grazing of the lower level $x=-1$, the lower residence time generically vanishes like a square root of the distance to the grazing parameter, so its derivative can become large. The precise coefficient is not needed here. This points to a narrow regime where the extra turn in $T$ can occur: the lower-zone visit is almost disappearing, while strong exterior damping continues to affect the return map. This is our explanation of the mechanism, not a global grazing theorem.

\section{How the witness was found}\label{sec:discovery}
We did not begin by solving for seven periodic orbits. The search started from an unsuccessful attempt to establish the simple residence-time shape that would imply the five-cycle conjecture. Let four successive crossing times on a genuine three-zone flight be $t_1<t_2<t_3<t_4$, with regions middle, lower, middle, upper. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:durations}
r=t_2-t_1,\quad s=t_3-t_2,\quad u=t_4-t_3,\quad v=t_1+2\pi-t_4.
\end{equation}
Here $T=r+u$. At a critical point of the response curve, \eqref{eq:key} gives $r+u=\rho$, removing one independent flight duration.

There is a direct way to test the sign of $T_\xi$. At a transverse crossing $x(t_i;\xi)=\pm1$, differentiate to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossdiff}
\frac{dt_i}{d\xi}=-\frac{z(t_i)}{\dot x(t_i)}.
\end{equation}
Along a middle flight $z$ grows by $e^{B\ell}$ over duration $\ell$; along an exterior flight it decays by $e^{-A\ell}$. Denoting $w_i=\dot x(t_i)\ne0$ and $z_1=z(t_1)>0$, substituting into $T=t_2-t_1+t_4-t_3$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
\boxed{\ T_\xi=z_1\,\mathcal H,\quad
\mathcal H=\frac1{w_1}-\frac{e^{Br}}{w_2}
+\frac{e^{Br-As}}{w_3}-\frac{e^{B(r+u)-As}}{w_4}.\ }
\end{equation}
On a critical orbit set $u=\rho-r$. Formula \eqref{eq:H} reduces an apparently global monotonicity claim to the sign of an explicit combination of four switching velocities. The matching equations of \cite[Section~3]{HuYuBound} reduce the admissible critical flights to a two-dimensional compatibility condition, so this sign can be investigated without shooting over every initial value.

\paragraph{From an attempted bound to a candidate counterexample.}
In numerical scans with relatively mild exterior damping, admissible critical flights tended to have $\mathcal H>0$. This encouraged the (incorrect) conjecture that $T_\xi$ had a universal sign there. Extending the scan to stronger exterior contraction revealed actual critical three-zone flights with $\mathcal H<0$. We then chose simple slopes $A=9$ and $B=3/2$. Tracking the numerically computed critical amplitude produced a shallow minimum near $\mu\simeq1.813584$, just below the symmetric threshold $\mu_c\simeq1.81363456$. We picked the rational value
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu}
\mu=\frac{181363}{100000}=1.81363
\end{equation}
between those two levels. These steps explain the discovery of the rational witness. The approximate critical values are exploratory outputs, not part of the theorem's hypotheses or proof.

\section{A concrete, rational seven-cycle witness}\label{sec:witness}
Our witness is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:witness}
\boxed{\ \dot x=-9x+\frac{21}{2}\sat(x)+\frac{181363}{100000}\sin t.\ }
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\dot x+g(x)=\mu\sin t$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gparts}
g(x)=\begin{cases}9x+21/2,&x<-1,\\-3x/2,&|x|\le1,\\9x-21/2,&x>1.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Note that the vector field is continuous and globally Lipschitz even at $x=\pm1$; existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence are therefore immediate. For initial value $x(0)=y$, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P}
P(y)=x(2\pi;0,y),\qquad D(y)=P(y)-y.
\end{equation}
Fixed points of $P$ are precisely the required periodic solutions.

\begin{theorem}[At least seven isolated periodic solutions]\label{thm:seven}
Equation \eqref{eq:witness} admits at least seven distinct isolated $2\pi$-periodic solutions on the phase cylinder. Consequently five is not a universal upper bound for the family \eqref{eq:main}.
\end{theorem}

\section{Proof: six sign tests, first-hit control, and symmetry}\label{sec:proof}
\subsection{Exact flights, not a numerical time-step approximation}
In a region where $g(x)=cx+d$, the flow from $x(t_0)=y_0$ is
\begin{align}\label{eq:flight}
\Phi_{c,d}(t;t_0,y_0)={}&e^{-c(t-t_0)}y_0-\frac dc\bigl(1-e^{-c(t-t_0)}\bigr)\\
&+\frac{\mu}{1+c^2}\Bigl[c\sin t-\cos t
-e^{-c(t-t_0)}(c\sin t_0-\cos t_0)\Bigr].\notag
\end{align}
Here $c=-3/2$ for the middle zone and $c=9$ for the outer zones. An exact orbit is obtained by joining these flows at the \emph{first} hitting times of $\pm1$. Only the transcendental evaluations and hitting-time brackets need interval enclosures.

There is a particularly simple observation that makes the event audit understandable.
\begin{lemma}[A threshold cannot be crossed repeatedly]\label{lem:cross}
Fix a threshold $\ell\in\{-1,1\}$ and a trajectory of \eqref{eq:witness}. At every transverse encounter with $x=\ell$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold}
\dot x=\mu\sin t-g(\ell)=\mu\sin t+\frac32\ell.
\end{equation}
For a \emph{periodic} trajectory, the level has at most one upward and one downward transverse crossing per period. For an arbitrary shooting trajectory, any change in the number of level crossings as the initial value varies can occur only at a tangency $\dot x=0$ at that level.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The times on the phase circle where the right side of \eqref{eq:threshold} is positive form one connected arc (or the empty set); the negative times form the complementary connected arc. Upward and downward level crossings of a continuous scalar trajectory necessarily alternate. On a periodic trajectory the crossings alternate cyclically; two upward crossings would require a downward crossing within the connected positive arc, which is impossible. The same applies to downward crossings. For an arbitrary shooting trajectory, transverse crossings persist under a small variation in the initial state by the implicit function theorem. A change of their number away from the endpoints of the time interval therefore requires a nontransverse encounter. In our shooting brackets, the end states at $t=0$ and $t=2\pi$ remain strictly away from both switching levels, so endpoint loss is also excluded.
\end{proof}

A second useful shape identity holds within each affine zone. If $\dot x+cx+d=\mu\sin t$ and $w(t)=e^{c(t-t_0)}\dot x(t)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shape}
\boxed{\ w'(t)=\mu e^{c(t-t_0)}\cos t.\ }
\end{equation}
Thus the velocity cannot develop a hidden oscillation inside a flight: it has a known monotonicity pattern on the arcs determined by $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$. The two identities also make it possible to check the absence of tangencies across an entire interval of initial values, rather than merely accepting the crossings returned by a numerical event detector.

Since $D$ is continuous, each interval

I_1=[-0.571,-0.567],\quad

For the first two pairs the certified zone sequence is
\[
\underbrace{\mathrm{middle}\to\mathrm{lower}\to\mathrm{middle}}_{\text{two $-1$ crossings}}
\to\underbrace{\mathrm{upper}\to\mathrm{middle}}_{\text{two $+1$ crossings}};
\]
for the last pair it is $\mathrm{middle}\to\mathrm{upper}\to\mathrm{middle}$. Each event is enclosed between rational decimal times at which the exact flight lies on opposite sides of the threshold; the crossing speed has a strict sign. The last interval has, additionally, a certified initial minimum strictly \emph{above} $-1$. The shape formula \eqref{eq:shape} rules out an earlier encounter at the endpoint trajectories.

The remaining concern is a possible hidden change of itinerary \emph{between} the rational shooting endpoints. This can now be excluded by a finite argument. A grazing at the level $\ell\in\{-1,1\}$ can occur only at one of the two phases solving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grazingphases}
\mu\sin t+\tfrac32\ell=0.
\end{equation}

Since $x(t;y)$ is strictly increasing in $y$ at every fixed time, each strict phase-level sign remains unchanged for all $y\in I_j$. No grazing is possible anywhere in these intervals, and no crossing can be lost at $t=0$ or $2\pi$, where the states are strictly inside the middle region. It follows that the zero set of $D$ within each $I_j$ is discrete: an analytic function with two nonzero endpoint values cannot vanish identically. We therefore obtain \emph{three distinct isolated fixed points}, not just three zeros belonging to a possible continuum of fixed points.

\subsection{Why they give seven rather than only three}
The forcing possesses the half-period reversal symmetry
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sym}
\mathcal Sx(t)=-x(t+\pi).
\end{equation}
The oddness of $g$ implies that $\mathcal S$ carries any periodic solution to another periodic solution. There is exactly one solution fixed by $\mathcal S$: the antisymmetric (central) solution.

To see existence and uniqueness directly, define
\[
Q(y)=x(\pi;0,y)+y.
\]
Scalar order makes $Q$ strictly increasing. Since $g(x)=9x+O(1)$ at both ends, $x(\pi;0,y)=e^{-9\pi}y+O(1)$ as $y\to\pm\infty$, so $Q$ has exactly one zero $y_c$. For that initial value, uniqueness and symmetry give $x_c(t+\pi)=-x_c(t)$ and therefore $2\pi$-periodicity. Our interval certificate gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qbound}
Q(-0.571)>0.01911577,
\end{equation}
so $y_c<-0.571$, below all three intervals in \eqref{eq:intervals}. By scalar order, the corresponding three periodic solutions lie strictly \emph{above} $x_c$ at every common phase. Their three reflections under $\mathcal S$ lie strictly \emph{below} $x_c$, and they are distinct from one another because the flow preserves strict initial-value order. Hence we have $3+1+3$ distinct periodic orbits.

It remains to check that the central orbit itself is isolated; otherwise it would not count as a limit cycle. The interval computation additionally proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centerbracket}
-0.579022471<y_c<-0.579022470
\end{equation}
and bounds the lower-zone residence $s$ during $[0,\pi]$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sbound}
s<0.448589954<\frac\pi7.
\end{equation}
Half-period antisymmetry makes its total exterior residence $2s$. The exact multiplier formula \eqref{eq:multiplier} therefore yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centerhyper}
\log P'(y_c)=\frac32(2\pi-2s)-9(2s)=3\pi-21s>0.
\end{equation}
Thus $P'(y_c)>1$; the central fixed point is hyperbolic and isolated. The symmetry map preserves isolation of each noncentral fixed point, completing the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:seven}. \qed

\section{What has and has not been established}
The proof does not require numerically counting the turning points in Figure~\ref{fig:response}; those estimates were only a guide for locating the parameters. The reason seven orbits appear is the additional reversal in the response curve near lower-level grazing. The reason we can \emph{prove} their existence is independent and simpler: three verified Poincar\'e sign changes and the half-period reflection symmetry.

There is an important limitation. The theorem proves $L(-9,3/2,1.81363)\ge7$ and disproves a universal upper bound of five. It does \emph{not} prove that seven is the maximum, nor does it give a classification of all three-zone response curves. The existing explicit global upper bound \cite{HuYuBound} is unaffected. It remains to determine how many such extra folds can occur as $A$, $B$, and $\mu$ vary, and whether seven can be attained on an explicitly bounded open set of parameters. Strict endpoint signs in fact persist under sufficiently small perturbations, but determining a useful rigorous parameter box is future work.

The computations deserve independent scrutiny before journal submission. The current sign certificate relies on a standard-conforming Python \texttt{Decimal} implementation with correctly directed rounding; it is not a formally verified proof kernel. A second implementation based on Arb/FLINT or exact rational intervals would be a valuable independent audit. We separate this software-level qualification from the mathematical implication of the displayed strict bounds.

\appendix
\section{How the interval sign certificate works}\label{app:certificate}
We record the computational contract so that the argument is verifiable without trusting the location of the plotted roots.

\paragraph{Rational input and function enclosures.}
Every shooting initial value and all parameter coefficients are exact rationals. We enclose $\pi$ using Machin's identity
\[
\pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239)
\]
with alternating rational arctangent series and first-omitted-term bounds. The values of $e^z$, $\sin z$, and $\cos z$ appearing in \eqref{eq:flight} are evaluated by interval Taylor sums with outward rounding. In all calls, $|z|<10$; truncation at order at least $160$ admits the uniform error enclosure
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
\bigl|\operatorname{remainder}\bigr|\le\frac{3^{10}10^{161}}{161!}<10^{-95}.
\end{equation}
Products use all four endpoint combinations, and interval reciprocals are allowed only when their denominator excludes zero. The actual decimal arithmetic is performed with 95 digits and separate downward and upward rounding modes.

\paragraph{Certified event times.}
The exploratory solver supplies decimal \emph{centers} for event times, not trusted root values. For each event, the checker tests an interval $[t_-,t_+]$ with rational endpoints. It evaluates the exact flight \eqref{eq:flight} at both endpoints and requires strict opposite signs relative to the relevant level $\ell=\pm1$; the interval crossing speed has a prescribed nonzero sign. The velocity shape identity \eqref{eq:shape} prevents a hidden earlier encounter. The four possible grazing phases \eqref{eq:grazingphases} are separately bracketed, and the signs of $x(t;y)-\ell$ at each phase are checked at both ends of every shooting interval. Scalar order then excludes grazing throughout the intervals. For two-crossing cases, the initial minimum is separately enclosed above $-1$. The exact state at the enclosed event is the known switching level $\ell$, so the next flight begins from the interval event time and that \emph{exact} state.
